Which I would ship, and why

A. Not because red cannot carry action — in this design it can. Once refusals are neutral panels, red never lands on a refusal, so the meaning collision you diagnosed is gone in B as well. The reason is narrower and it is measurable.

5.9:1 against 3.7:1. Both clear the 3:1 floor for interface chrome. Neither clears 4.5:1 for body text, so neither colours a sentence — but the action here is a 1px outline and 11px small caps, the two places where luminance is all you have.
Glare and a respirator visor. Sun through a workshop door plus a scratched screen eats the darkest hues first. Red is the lowest-luminance saturated hue available; it is the first thing to disappear.
Four people, then more. Red-green deficiency runs at roughly one man in twelve. Red on near-black is the worst case for it; violet is not.
Red is worth keeping in reserve. Nothing in this flow is destructive today. Voiding an authorisation or deleting a signed job card will be, and a colour that means only that is worth having unspent.

The cost of A is real but small: this is an internal tool for four people who know whose app it is. Red stays the identity — logo, app icon, splash, the mission-control dashboard. It just stops being the thing you tap.

IF YOU SHIP B ANYWAY
It is safe under two rules, and only two. Red appears on the one action and nowhere else — never on a refusal, a waiting panel, a status chip or a step marker. And red is never a fill: outline only, so the brand reads as a line and glare has less to take. Swapping is one token either way.
